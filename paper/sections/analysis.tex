\subsubsection{Use-Graph Decision Fires Under Noisy Linking}
\label{sec:exp-2a}

Under MetaQA's perfect linking, (D1) never fires (top-1 cosine ${\ge}0.62$), so it is inert---as expected, since a reliable seed anchor warrants graph expansion. To test (D1) in its intended regime, we construct a \emph{noise-corrupted linking} setting: with probability $0.5$ we truncate each bracketed entity to its first token (e.g., ``[Michelle Trachtenberg]''$\to$``[Michelle]''), then re-link by embedding. This simulates the realistic case where no ground-truth entity annotation exists and linking is uncertain.

Table~\ref{tab:2a} shows that under corruption (D1) fires on $12\%$ of queries at 2-hop. \method{} now diverges from the \emph{w/o (D1)} ablation: it cuts cost by an additional $10\%$ (835 vs.\ 933 \Toks{}) and improves F1 by $25\%$ over Fixed (0.150 vs.\ 0.120). Notably, skipping the graph on low-confidence queries \emph{raises} accuracy, because expanding from a wrong seed propagates noise. This validates (D1) as a conditional contribution: it is free under good linking and protective under bad linking.

\subsubsection{Hyperparameter Sensitivity}
\label{sec:exp-sens}

\Cref{fig:sensitivity} sweeps the three controller hyperparameters at 1.5B/2-hop ($n{=}100$). The early-stop margin $\delta$ is stable on $[0.01,0.05]$ (F1$\approx$0.214) and degrades only at $\delta{=}0.2$ (F1$=$0.175), so the default $\delta{=}0.05$ sits in a wide robust plateau. The base beam $B$ trades cost for accuracy monotonically ($B{=}2$: F1$=$0.144; $B{=}8$: F1$=$0.271), and $B{=}4$ is a reasonable default. The use-graph threshold $\theta_{\text{low}}$ is inert under perfect linking (all values give identical results), confirming it only activates under noisy linking as designed. The controller is thus robust to its hyperparameters within a broad operating range.

\subsubsection{Honest Boundaries}
\label{sec:exp-boundary}

We deliberately report settings where \method{} does \emph{not} dominate, as they delimit the method's applicability and increase the credibility of the positive results.

\textbf{Trivial queries trade accuracy for cost.} At 1.5B/1-hop, \method{} lowers cost by $22\%$ but F1 drops from 0.341 to 0.332 and Hit@1 from 0.630 to 0.556. Early stopping prunes a third hop that, for easy lookups, occasionally supplies a corroborating fact. The ablation shows \emph{w/o (D2)} actually edges out the full controller here (F1 0.345 vs.\ 0.332) because adaptive beam narrows exploration on confident hops. This is a controllable trade-off: a practitioner who cannot tolerate any accuracy loss on easy queries can disable (D3) for 1-hop regimes, or raise $\delta$. We do not claim \method{} is uniformly better; we claim it is Pareto-better \emph{where retrieval cost matters and queries are non-trivial}.

\textbf{Weak judge + high depth makes graph retrieval harmful.} At 1.5B/3-hop, No-Graph (F1$=$0.109) outperforms graph retrieval (\method{} F1$=$0.091). The 1.5B judge is unreliable at depth 3, so multi-hop expansion propagates scoring errors faster than it accumulates evidence. This is a property of the \emph{judge}, not the controller: the 7B judge restores graph utility (the 3-hop gap narrows and reverses in direction, though absolute F1 remains low because 3-hop MetaQA is intrinsically hard for open 7B models). The practical takeaway is that \method{} (and GraphRAG generally) requires a judge whose reliability scales with hop depth.

These boundaries are consistent with the theory: Proposition~\ref{prop:cost} guarantees cost savings unconditionally, while accuracy gains depend on whether the pruned hops carry signal or noise---exactly the distinction that separates the 2-hop/7B win from the 1-hop/1.5B trade-off.
