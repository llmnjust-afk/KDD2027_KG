\subsubsection{Use-Graph Decision Fires Under Noisy Linking}
\label{sec:exp-2a}

Under MetaQA's perfect linking, (D1) never fires (top-1 cosine ${\ge}0.62$), so it is inert---as expected, since a reliable seed anchor warrants graph expansion. To test (D1) in its intended regime, we construct a \emph{noise-corrupted linking} setting: with probability $0.5$ we truncate each bracketed entity to its first token (e.g., ``[Michelle Trachtenberg]''$\to$``[Michelle]''), then re-link by embedding. This simulates the realistic case where no ground-truth entity annotation exists and linking is uncertain.

Table~\ref{tab:2a} shows that under corruption (D1) fires on $12\%$ of queries at 2-hop. Two comparisons must be kept distinct. \emph{Relative to Fixed}, the full \method{} controller improves F1 (0.150 vs.\ 0.120) and cuts cost by $25.8\%$ (835 vs.\ 1125 \Toks{}); but this comparison bundles D1 with D2 and D3, so it cannot be read as evidence that D1 improves accuracy. \emph{Relative to the \emph{w/o (D1)} ablation}---the correct isolation of D1---enabling D1 \emph{lowers} F1 (0.150 vs.\ 0.167) while cutting cost by $10.5\%$ (835 vs.\ 933 \Toks{}). Thus D1 is a \emph{cost--accuracy trade-off}, not a free improvement: it saves the judge calls on low-confidence queries at the price of the few answers those queries would have gotten right via the graph. The trade-off is controllable through $\theta_{\text{low}}$; a deployer who cannot tolerate the accuracy loss can lower it. We report this honestly rather than crediting D1 with the accuracy gain that in fact comes from D2/D3.

\subsubsection{Signal Quality: Is the Stopping Signal Safe?}
\label{sec:exp-signal}

A key question is whether D3's stopping signal (top-score gain $\le\delta$) is a reliable proxy for ``the next hop is unnecessary.'' We measure this directly: at 7B/2-hop, queries where D3 stopped early have a correctness rate of $56.0\%$, \emph{identical} to queries where D3 continued ($56.0\%$)---i.e.\ $P(\text{correct}\mid\text{stopped}) \approx P(\text{correct}\mid\text{continued})$, confirming the stopped hops were genuinely uninformative. At 7B/1-hop the picture is even stronger: stopped queries are \emph{more} accurate ($93.3\%$ vs.\ $86.4\%$), because the pruned hops carried judge noise. At 1.5B/3-hop the proxy breaks (stopped $21.2\%$ vs.\ continued $26.2\%$), consistent with the boundary analysis. This validates the accuracy-preservation condition of \Cref{sec:stop-theory}: the stopping signal is safe precisely when the judge is reliable enough that a flat top-score trajectory implies the next hop adds no answer-relevant evidence.

\subsubsection{Honest Boundaries}
\label{sec:exp-boundary}

We deliberately report two settings where \method{} does \emph{not} win, as they delimit its applicability. \textbf{(i) Trivial queries:} at 1.5B/1-hop, \method{} lowers cost by $22\%$ but F1 drops $0.341{\to}0.332$ (and \emph{w/o (D2)} edges out the full controller, F1 $0.345$), because early stopping occasionally prunes a corroborating hop; a practitioner intolerant of this can raise $\delta$. \textbf{(ii) Weak judge at depth:} at 1.5B/3-hop, No-Graph (F1 $0.109$) beats \method{} (F1 $0.091$)---the 1.5B judge is too unreliable at depth 3, so expansion propagates scoring errors faster than evidence; the 7B judge restores graph utility. Both cases match the accounting in \Cref{prop:cost}: cost savings follow whenever (D1)/(D3) fires, but accuracy is preserved only when the pruned hops carry noise rather than signal---the distinction separating the 2-hop/7B win from the 1-hop/1.5B trade-off.
