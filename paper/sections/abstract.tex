Graph retrieval-augmented generation (GraphRAG) answers multi-hop questions by iteratively expanding a knowledge-graph frontier and invoking a large language model (LLM) as a relevance judge at each hop---an expensive budget applied uniformly to every query. A natural hypothesis, pursued by a growing body of work, is that a \emph{training-free, query-adaptive} controller can cut this cost by spending more retrieval only on hard queries. We test this hypothesis rigorously across three orthogonal adaptation axes---retrieval \emph{depth} (how many hops), \emph{judge budget} (whether to invoke the LLM judge), and \emph{judge type} (embedding vs.\ LLM relevance scoring)---on three KGQA benchmarks (MetaQA, ComplexWebQuestions, WebQSP) with 1.5B, 7B, and 14B judges, and under both a naive and a stronger ToG-2.0-style retrieval engine. Our central finding is negative and consistent: on every axis, a \emph{tuned global} budget lies on or above the accuracy--cost Pareto frontier of every training-free per-query adaptive policy we construct, including an oracle-depth baseline that cheats by using the true hop count. To explain \emph{why}, we conduct an oracle analysis: for each axis we measure the accuracy of an omniscient policy that makes the optimal per-query choice. The oracle upside is large---per-query judge selection would raise F1 from $0.41$ to $0.47$ using only $27\%$ of the LLM calls---\emph{yet no realizable policy captures it}: gap-based routing, threshold rules, and trained routers on question and pipeline features all fall below the always-on baseline. We argue this reflects a \emph{predictability ceiling}: the information that determines the optimal per-query decision---whether an extra hop or a stronger judge would change the answer---is revealed only \emph{after} the expensive computation, not before it, so inference-time signals cannot anticipate it. The consistency of this result across three adaptation axes, three benchmarks, three judge scales, and two engine strengths---including a strong-engine ablation that rules out the possibility that adaptation fails merely because our retrieval engine is weak---is the paper's core evidence. We conclude that useful adaptation in GraphRAG will require learned policies with post-retrieval signals, not training-free inference-time heuristics, and provide a tuned-global-budget baseline and an open, reproducible testbed for future work.
