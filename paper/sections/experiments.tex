\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:exp-setup}
We use three KGQA benchmarks. \textbf{MetaQA}~\cite{zhang2017variational} provides 1/2/3-hop questions over the WikiMovies KG; we build a \emph{mixed-depth stream} of equal parts 1/2/3-hop questions sharing one KG to model a realistic heterogeneous workload. \textbf{ComplexWebQuestions (CWQ)}~\cite{talmor2018knowledgebase} provides naturally heterogeneous questions (1--4 hop compositions, comparatives, superlatives) over Freebase, with a pre-extracted subgraph per query (avg.\ 3{,}400 triples); this tests whether natural difficulty heterogeneity---rather than MetaQA's templated splits---makes per-query adaptation pay off. \textbf{WebQSP} provides natural web questions over Freebase; following recent KGQA work we use the dense per-query subgraphs released by RoG~\cite{luo2023reasoning} (avg.\ 4{,}135 triples/query), a third and independent testbed whose question style and graph density differ sharply from the other two. The judge/generator is Qwen2.5-\{1.5B, 7B, 14B\}-Instruct; embeddings use \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}. We report F1, Hit@1, and input tokens/query, all on identical query subsets per comparison.

\subsection{Axis 1: Adaptive Depth Loses to a Tuned Global $K$}
\label{sec:exp-depth}

We first ask whether adapting the hop count per query beats a tuned global $K$. \Cref{tab:depth-mixed} reports the full Pareto comparison on the MetaQA mixed stream (7B, $n{=}240$, identical subset). A tuned Fixed-$K{=}2,B{=}8$ achieves F1 $0.478$ at $1101$ \Toks{} and is \emph{non-dominated}; it beats every depth-adaptive policy---marginal-gain stopping, patience, margin, confidence-threshold---and, strikingly, beats the \textbf{oracle-depth} policy ($0.444$ at $999$ \Toks{}) that uses the ground-truth hop count. The reason is diagnostic: measuring per-hop F1, we find that on MetaQA the third hop injects judge noise even for 3-hop questions, so running two hops for \emph{every} query dominates matching each query's true depth. On CWQ the pattern is even starker: per-hop F1 is essentially flat in $K$ (F1$\approx$0.08 at $K{=}1,2,3$), so retrieval depth barely affects the answer and the cheapest fixed budget wins. Across both benchmarks and all three judge scales (1.5B/7B/14B; App.~A), no depth-adaptive policy is non-dominated.

The mechanism behind this failure is that the optimal $K$ is a near-global constant (2 on MetaQA, 1 on CWQ), and the per-hop signals available before running hop $k$ do not predict whether hop $k$ will help: at hop 0 the top relevance score is near $1.0$ for both 1-hop and 2-hop queries (the seed's direct neighbor is always relevant), so no threshold separates them (\Cref{sec:exp-oracle}).

\subsection{Axes 2 \& 3: Adaptive Judge Budget and Type}
\label{sec:exp-judge}

We next adapt the \emph{judge}, and use this axis to run a critical robustness check. \Cref{tab:judge-depth} reports embedding vs.\ LLM judge by depth under two engines: our naive engine (candidates truncated in traversal order) and a stronger ToG-2.0-style engine that ranks all candidates by embedding similarity before judging. Under the \emph{naive} engine, the free embedding judge appears to win in three of four regimes---but this is an artifact: traversal-order truncation hides answer triples from the LLM judge on dense graphs. Under the \emph{strong} engine the LLM judge improves sharply (CWQ: $0.024\!\to\!0.085$; 2-hop: $0.285\!\to\!0.248$ with far lower cost) and \emph{outperforms} the embedding judge in three of four regimes (all but the trivial 1-hop). We therefore do not claim the LLM judge is dispensable; we report the ablation to establish that our downstream negative result on adaptation is measured on a properly strong engine, not a crippled one.

Given a properly strong engine, can a policy adapt the judge per query? \textsc{AdaptiveJudge} (Axis 2) skips the LLM when the embedding score is confident. On the MetaQA mixed stream it is Pareto-dominated by the tuned global Fixed-$K{=}2,B{=}8$ (\Cref{tab:depth-mixed}). Adapting the judge \emph{type} per query (Axis 3) is a routing problem, analyzed next; it is precisely because the LLM and embedding judges are each better on different queries (\Cref{tab:judge-depth}) that per-query routing has a large \emph{oracle} upside---which, as we show, is nonetheless unrealizable.

\subsection{The Predictability Ceiling: Oracle Analysis}
\label{sec:exp-oracle}

Why does every adaptive policy fail? We test whether the bottleneck is our policy design or a fundamental limit, by measuring an \emph{oracle} that makes the optimal per-query judge choice. \Cref{tab:oracle} reports the result on the MetaQA mixed stream (7B, test $n{=}150$). The embedding and LLM judges are genuinely \emph{complementary}: the oracle that picks the better judge per query reaches F1 $0.474$ using only $27\%$ LLM calls---far above always-LLM ($0.411$) and always-embedding ($0.329$). The upside of perfect per-query adaptation is large.

\input{sections/tab_oracle}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\columnwidth]{figures/fig_ceiling.pdf}
\caption{The predictability ceiling. The oracle (red) that picks the better judge per query has large headroom over Always-LLM (dashed), but every realizable policy---gap routing and trained routers---falls at or below Always-LLM. The shaded band is upside no inference-time signal reaches.}
\label{fig:ceiling}
\end{figure}

Yet no realizable policy captures it. Gap-based routing (route to the LLM when the hop-0 embedding gap is small) reaches only $0.354$---\emph{below} always-LLM. A trained logistic-regression router on the question embedding reaches $0.370$; on cheap pipeline signals it degrades to $0.329$ (always-embedding). Every deployable policy falls below the trivial always-LLM baseline, despite the oracle's large headroom.

One might object that these routers are underpowered. We therefore stress-test the ceiling with progressively richer feature sets and stronger model classes, all evaluated with proper $5$-fold cross-validated predictions on the $n{=}300$ query pool ($21.7\%$ of queries favor the LLM judge). We give the router the full hop-0 score distribution (top-1, top-2, gap, mean, candidate count) and the retrieval depth, and train logistic regression, a two-layer MLP ($256\!\to\!128$), and a $300$-tree random forest. The best cross-validated AUROC for predicting whether the LLM judge wins is only $0.559$---barely above the $0.5$ chance line---and the induced routed F1 never exceeds $0.379$, still below always-LLM ($0.413$) and far below the oracle ($0.479$). Feature richness and model capacity do not move the ceiling: the discriminative information is simply absent from any pre-computation signal.

We interpret this as a \emph{predictability ceiling}. The label a router must predict---whether the LLM judge would yield a better answer than the embedding judge for this query---depends on whether the correct answer triple happens to rank highly under each scorer, which is determined only by \emph{running} the scorers and the downstream generator. No signal computed \emph{before} the expensive computation (question text, entity-linking confidence, hop-0 score distribution) carries this information, because difficulty in GraphRAG is a property of the query--pipeline \emph{interaction} revealed post hoc, not a predictable property of the query. This is the same reason depth adaptation fails: whether hop $k{+}1$ helps is knowable only after running it. The three axes share one root cause.

\subsection{Cross-Family Robustness}
\label{sec:exp-family}

A natural concern is that our conclusions are specific to the Qwen judge family. We repeat the mixed-stream depth comparison on two additional families---Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3---under the strong engine (\Cref{tab:family}). The negative result is unchanged: on all three families the tuned global Fixed-$K{=}2,B{=}8$ dominates the best training-free adaptive policy (Qwen $0.478$ vs.\ $0.394$; Llama $0.248$ vs.\ $0.239$; Mistral $0.569$ vs.\ $0.453$), and the Pareto structure is identical (a cheap embedding-only point and a tuned $K{=}2$ high-accuracy point bound the frontier). Combined with the 1.5B/7B/14B scale sweep, this shows the predictability ceiling holds across judge scale \emph{and} family.

\subsection{A Third Benchmark: WebQSP}
\label{sec:exp-webqsp}

Our evidence so far comes from MetaQA (synthetic multi-hop over WikiMovies) and CWQ. To test whether the ceiling is an artifact of these two datasets, we add WebQSP, whose RoG Freebase subgraphs are markedly denser (on average $4{,}135$ triples per query versus a few dozen for MetaQA) and whose questions are natural web queries rather than templated multi-hop chains. We run the identical depth/judge comparison under the strong engine on $n{=}120$ WebQSP questions (\Cref{tab:webqsp}). The pattern is unchanged: the tuned global Fixed-$K{=}2,B{=}8$ point reaches $0.338$ F1 and again dominates \textsc{AdaptiveJudge} at $0.293$ ($\Delta{=}{+}0.045$), which in turn merely interpolates between the cheap embedding-only policy ($0.251$) and the tuned point. Most tellingly, the per-query oracle over depth $K\!\in\!\{1,2,3\}$ reaches only $0.324$---\emph{below} the tuned Fixed-$K{=}2$ budget itself. On WebQSP there is no depth headroom to capture at all: a single well-chosen global depth already matches the best any per-query depth policy could achieve, so adaptation cannot help even in principle. That the predictability ceiling reappears on a dataset with an order-of-magnitude denser graph and a different question distribution indicates it is a structural property of GraphRAG retrieval, not a quirk of any one benchmark.

\subsection{Multi-Seed Statistical Validation}
\label{sec:exp-multiseed}

To ensure the gap between the tuned global budget and the best adaptive policy is not a single-run artifact, we scale the main mixed-stream comparison to $n{=}500$ per seed over three independent query subsamples (\Cref{tab:multiseed}). The ranking is stable across seeds (std $\le 0.013$), and a paired bootstrap on the pooled $1{,}494$ queries gives $\Delta$F1${=}{+}0.055$ for Fixed-$K{=}2,B{=}8$ over \textsc{AdaptiveJudge}, with a $95\%$ CI of $[{+}0.039,{+}0.070]$ and $P(\Delta{\le}0){<}10^{-4}$. The tuned global budget is thus \emph{significantly} better than the best training-free adaptive policy, not merely better on average.

\subsection{Cost--Accuracy Trade-off}
\label{sec:exp-cost}

A final rebuttal remains: perhaps adaptation is worthwhile because it trades a negligible amount of accuracy for a large efficiency gain. We measure this directly on the mixed stream under the strong engine, recording wall-clock latency, the number of LLM relevance-judge calls (the dominant marginal cost), and F1 for three representative policies (\Cref{tab:cost}). We report the trade-off honestly. \textsc{AdaptiveJudge} \emph{is} cheaper than the tuned global point---$1.30$s versus $3.16$s per query and $0.31$ versus $2.00$ judge calls---because it escalates to the LLM judge only when the embedding probe is uncertain. What it is not, is accuracy-competitive or Pareto-dominant: it scores $0.371$ F1, still below the tuned Fixed-$K{=}2,B{=}8$ point at $0.380$, while the pure embedding policy already offers an even cheaper operating point ($0.89$s, zero judge calls) at $0.323$. Adaptation therefore only \emph{interpolates} between two tuned static budgets on the cost--accuracy plane; it does not reach the ``best of both worlds'' the oracle's headroom (\Cref{sec:exp-oracle}) suggests should exist. This is exactly what the predictability ceiling predicts: because the router cannot tell in advance which queries need the LLM judge, escalating on the embedding gap saves calls on many queries that would have benefited and spends calls on many that would not, landing strictly inside the static frontier rather than above it. Efficiency does not rescue per-query adaptation; it merely reproduces, at a worse accuracy, a point a practitioner could pick by tuning a single global budget.
