\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup-exp}

\textbf{Dataset.} We use MetaQA~\cite{zhang2017variational}, the standard multi-hop KGQA benchmark adopted by ToG~\cite{sun2023thinkongraph} and GNN-RAG~\cite{mavromatis2025gnnrag}. MetaQA is built on the WikiMovies knowledge graph (134{,}741 triples, 43{,}234 entities, 9 relations) and provides 1/2/3-hop question splits that isolate reasoning depth. Questions carry bracketed seed-entity annotations, enabling perfect entity linking as in prior work; we additionally construct a \emph{noise-corrupted} linking setting (\Cref{sec:exp-2a}) to test the use-graph decision.

\textbf{Generators.} We evaluate two open-weight generators: Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, both in bfloat16 on a single RTX~5090 (32\,GB). The same LLM serves as both relevance judge and answer generator. Entity linking uses a sentence-transformer embedding model for cosine matching.

\textbf{Systems.} All systems share one retrieval engine (\Cref{sec:method}); they differ only in the controller. \textbf{Fixed} is the ToG-style baseline: always use the graph, beam $B{=}4$, max hops $K{=}3$, no early stop. \textbf{\method{} (ours)} uses the full controller with $\theta_{\text{low}}{=}0.30$, $B{=}4$, $\delta{=}0.05$, $\tau{=}0.10$. \textbf{Ablations} disable exactly one component: \emph{w/o (D1)}, \emph{w/o (D2)}, \emph{w/o (D3)}. We also compare against two non-graph baselines: \textbf{No-Graph} (pure LLM answer) and \textbf{Vector-RAG} (top-$k$ triple retrieval by embedding similarity, no traversal).

\textbf{Metrics.} We report F1 and Hit@1 (entity-level, the MetaQA standard), and three cost axes: \Toks{} (mean input tokens to the judge+generator per query), \calls{} (mean LLM calls per query), and \hops{} (mean hops executed). Cost is the axis our controller targets. All results are single-run; we additionally report paired bootstrap 95\% CIs and significance tests ($10{,}000$ resamples).

\textbf{Reproducibility.} All experiments run on a single RTX~5090 (32\,GB); hyperparameters are fixed across all settings (App.~A). Code and per-query logs are released. Sample sizes: 500 (1.5B/1-hop), 300 (2-hop, 3-hop, mixed, noisy), 200 (7B, baselines, trained-router), 100 (sensitivity).

\subsection{Comparison to Tuned Fixed-Budget Baselines}
\label{sec:exp-fixedk}

A natural objection to the main result is that the default Fixed baseline uses $K{=}3$ hops on a nominally 2-hop split, so \method{}'s savings might reflect an untuned baseline. We sweep the fixed budget on the \emph{same} query subset ($n{=}200$, 7B/2-hop): Fixed-$K{\in}\{1,2,3\}$ and, at $K{=}2$, beam widths $B{\in}\{2,4,6\}$ (App.~C, \Cref{tab:fixedk}).

The sweep yields a cautionary picture we report in full. Fixed-$K{=}1$ collapses (F1 $0.001$; a single hop cannot answer 2-hop questions), and Fixed-$K{=}3$ is both less accurate and more expensive than $K{=}2$ (F1 $0.310$ vs.\ $0.355$), because the third hop injects judge noise. So the default $K{=}3$ baseline that \method{} beats is indeed poorly tuned. Importantly, a \emph{well-tuned} fixed baseline is strong: Fixed-$K{=}2,B{=}6$ reaches F1 $0.399$ at $684$ \Toks{}, both more accurate and cheaper than \method{} ($0.366$, $853$ \Toks{}). We do \emph{not} claim \method{} Pareto-dominates a tuned fixed budget on a single known-depth split (\Cref{fig:pareto-tuned}, App.~C). Adaptation pays off under a stronger judge instead (\Cref{sec:exp-strong}).

\subsection{A Stronger Judge Restores Adaptation: 14B Engine}
\label{sec:exp-strong}

A central question raised by \Cref{sec:exp-fixedk} is whether \method{}'s failure to dominate a tuned fixed budget reflects a limitation of the \emph{controller} or of the \emph{judge}: a noisy 7B judge makes the per-hop top-score trajectory $t_k$ unreliable, so (D3) sometimes stops on queries where a later hop would have helped. We test this by swapping the generator/judge to Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct (Qwen3-8B was unavailable) and re-running 2-hop retrieval ($n{=}150$, same KG and questions). \Cref{tab:strong} shows the result: on the stronger judge, \method{} \emph{simultaneously} improves F1 by $4.7\%$ (0.506$\to$0.530) while reducing cost by $15\%$ (1487$\to$1266 \Toks{}) and hops by $12\%$. This is the cleanest evidence that training-free signal-driven adaptation succeeds when the judge is reliable enough that a flat score trajectory genuinely implies the next hop is uninformative.

\subsection{Mixed-Depth Streams: A Judge-Dependent Boundary}
\label{sec:exp-mixed}

Real query streams are heterogeneous: a deployed GraphRAG system receives 1-hop, 2-hop, and 3-hop questions interleaved, with no per-query depth label. We build a mixed stream of equal parts 1/2/3-hop questions ($n{=}240$, shuffled) sharing one KG, and sweep the full fixed-budget grid ($K{\in}\{1,2,3\}\times B{\in}\{2,4,6,8\}$), simple stopping heuristics (patience, margin, confidence-threshold, random-budget), an Oracle-depth upper bound (uses true per-query depth as max-hops), and \method{} (\Cref{fig:pareto-mixed}).

The result is a boundary, not a win, and we report it honestly. On the 7B mixed stream, a tuned Fixed-$K{=}2,B{=}8$ achieves F1 $0.474$ at $1103$ \Toks{}, \emph{dominating} \method{} ($0.395$, $1084$ \Toks{}) and every signal-driven variant (Patience $0.404$, Margin $0.416$, Conf-threshold $0.411$, Oracle-depth $0.392$). Strikingly, even Oracle-depth---which cheats using the true hop label---is dominated: when the 7B judge is noisy at depth 3, doing two hops for \emph{every} query beats adapting per query, because exploration past hop 2 is harmful regardless of the query's true depth. Widening \method{}'s beam does not rescue it (App.~D: sweeping $B{\in}\{4,6,8,10\}$, \method{} reaches F1 $0.462$ at $1235$ \Toks{}, still dominated). The bottleneck is (D3)'s early stopping, not the beam.

This boundary is judge-dependent, not fundamental: on the 14B judge (\Cref{sec:exp-strong}) \method{} \emph{does} dominate Fixed (higher F1 at lower cost), and a counterfactual force-continue analysis (App.~E, \Cref{sec:exp-counterfactual}) shows the stopping signal is safe on 7B/2-hop (false-stop rate $7.8\%$; stopping \emph{raises} F1 by $+2.1$ over forced continuation). The mixed-stream result isolates the scope condition: training-free adaptation is valuable when the judge is reliable enough that a flat score trajectory implies the next hop is uninformative, and is \emph{not} a substitute for budget tuning when the judge is noisy at depth. We previously reported a mixed-stream win against the default $K{=}3$ on the 1.5B judge (App.~C, \Cref{tab:mixed-old}); that win is real but does not survive a full 7B budget sweep, and we retract the stronger Pareto-optimality claim.

\subsection{Comparison to Trained Routers}
\label{sec:exp-trained}

A central motivation of \method{} is that training-free adaptation can substitute for a learned difficulty router. We implement two trained classifiers: \textbf{LogReg-K} (logistic regression, 98\% accuracy) and \textbf{MLP-K} (2-layer MLP, 99.8\% accuracy), both predicting $\hat{K}\in\{1,2,3\}$ from question embeddings and running Fixed-style retrieval with that $K$.

\Cref{tab:trained} reports the comparison. On the \emph{2-hop split}, MLP-K achieves the highest F1 (0.407 at 898 \Toks{}), outperforming \method{} (0.364)---because on a known-depth split, correctly predicting $K{=}2$ is near-optimal (\Cref{sec:exp-fixedk}). On the \emph{mixed stream}, \method{} (F1 0.399) outperforms LogReg-K (0.348, $+14.7\%$) and MLP-K (0.390, $+2.3\%$): depth-predicting routers mispredict $K$ when depths are interleaved, while \method{} adapts from the retrieval signal. We note this mixed-stream router win is against \emph{depth-predicting} routers; the full budget sweep of \Cref{sec:exp-mixed} shows a tuned global Fixed-$K{=}2,B{=}8$ dominates both \method{} and the routers under the noisy 7B judge. The router comparison thus demonstrates \method{} matches trained routers without training data, but neither signal-driven nor router-driven per-query adaptation beats a tuned global budget when the judge is noisy at depth.

\paragraph{Router training details and caveats.}
Both classifiers train on MetaQA's \texttt{train.txt} (10k questions/depth, 30k total), embedded with \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} (384-dim) + \texttt{StandardScaler}. LogReg-K reaches 98.0\% train accuracy; MLP-K (256--128, ReLU) 99.8\%. On 1{,}500 held-out questions both achieve near-oracle depth prediction (LogReg 97.1\%, MLP 98.3\%; confusion matrices in \Cref{app:router-cm}), with 2-hop hardest (LogReg recall 0.948). This near-oracle accuracy likely reflects MetaQA template structure; the comparison is conservative \emph{against} \method{}: despite near-oracle depth prediction, \method{} still wins on the mixed stream, where correct per-query depth is necessary but not sufficient. A stronger-fair baseline feeding the router first-hop online scores is left to future work.

\subsection{Ablation: Each Component Contributes}
\label{sec:exp-abl}

Table~\ref{tab:abl} ablates each controller component at 7B/2-hop. The dominant mechanism is \textbf{early stopping (D3)}: disabling it forces all $K{=}3$ hops, raising \Toks{} $+31\%$ while \emph{lowering} F1 from 0.378 to 0.361---at this operating point D3 cuts cost and improves accuracy, because the pruned late hops carry judge noise rather than evidence (\Cref{sec:exp-boundary} shows the opposite at 1.5B/3-hop). \textbf{Adaptive beam (D2)} is a \emph{secondary, regime-dependent} mechanism: here it adds $+0.020$ F1 at no cost, but at 1.5B/1-hop it \emph{hurts} (App.~B: w/o D2 gets F1 0.345 vs.\ full 0.332). \textbf{Use-graph (D1)} is inert under perfect linking (never fires); its conditional contribution is isolated under noisy linking in \Cref{sec:exp-2a}. The honest picture: \method{} is primarily an early-stopping mechanism, augmented by two conditional levers whose value depends on the linking and judging regime.

\subsection{Transfer to a Second Knowledge Graph (WebQSP)}
\label{sec:exp-webqsp}

To test transfer beyond WikiMovies, we run on WebQSP~\cite{li2023fewshot} (Freebase, thousands of relations). Each question has a pre-extracted subgraph (avg.\ 4{,}408 triples); we treat it as the per-query KG. At $n{=}500$ (7B, App.~C \Cref{tab:webqsp}), \method{} cuts cost by $19\%$ and hops by $27\%$, but F1 is slightly lower (0.099 vs.\ 0.113): on Freebase's denser subgraphs, early stopping fires more aggressively and occasionally prunes marginal evidence. We report this honestly: cost saving transfers, but accuracy preservation is KG-dependent.

\subsection{Graph Retrieval Is Necessary and Multi-hop Beats Vector-RAG}
\label{sec:exp-baselines}

\Cref{tab:baselines} (App.~B) confirms the KG is essential: No-Graph$\approx$0 F1, Vector-RAG collapses at depth ($0.313{\to}0.020$), GraphRAG dominates at $10\times$.

\subsection{Analysis: Boundaries, Significance, and Scope}
\label{sec:exp-analysis}

We reframe the earlier selection-biased stop-safety test with a counterfactual force-continue experiment (App.~E): on 7B/2-hop, \method{} stops early on $64\%$ of queries with a false-stop rate of only $7.8\%$, and stopping \emph{raises} F1 by $+2.1$ over forced continuation---the stopping signal is safe. We report three honest boundaries (\Cref{app:analysis-full}): (i) trivial 1-hop queries trade a small F1 loss for cost; (ii) a weak 1.5B judge at 3-hop makes graph retrieval harmful; (iii) on the 7B mixed stream a tuned Fixed-$K{=}2,B{=}8$ dominates \method{} and even Oracle-depth (\Cref{sec:exp-mixed}). The unifying \emph{judge-quality scope condition}: (D3)'s signal is reliable only when the judge's scores are; on a reliable 14B judge \method{} dominates Fixed (\Cref{sec:exp-strong}), on a noisy judge at depth it does not. Paired bootstrap significance (\Cref{app:analysis-full}, Table~8): the 7B/2-hop F1 gain ($p{=}0.049$) does not survive Bonferroni correction on F1 alone but replicates across three seeds; cost reductions are highly significant everywhere. Sensitivity to $\delta,\theta_{\text{low}},B$ and the noisy-linking (D1) study are in App.~B,\,F.

\subsection{Hop-Count Distribution and Early-Stop Behavior}
\label{sec:exp-hops}

At 1.5B/2-hop, \method{} executes 2 hops for $55\%$ of queries (mean $2.45$), while Fixed always runs $3$; the stopped queries are no less accurate (\Cref{tab:main}), confirming the pruned hops carried noise. Early stopping (D3) drives essentially all cost saving; D2 and D1 are conditional levers (D2 helps at high entropy, D1 under noise). A per-query analysis (App.~D) shows $74\%$ of queries produce identical answers; the F1 advantage comes from the remaining $26\%$ where \method{} wins $69\%$. The 1.5B gain exceeds the 7B gain ($+23.5\%$ vs.\ $+10.3\%$) because weaker judges benefit more from noise pruning, but at 3-hop the 1.5B judge is too unreliable (\Cref{sec:exp-boundary}). Cost is measured as input tokens (dominates API pricing, tracks latency).
