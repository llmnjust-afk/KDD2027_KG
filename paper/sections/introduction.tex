\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Large language models (LLMs) answer knowledge-intensive questions more faithfully when grounded in external evidence, and the graph-structured variant of retrieval-augmented generation---GraphRAG---reasons over multi-hop paths in a knowledge graph (KG)~\cite{sun2023thinkongraph,mavromatis2025gnnrag,sun2025searchongraph}. The canonical retrieval loop, exemplified by Think-on-Graph (ToG)~\cite{sun2023thinkongraph} and its successors~\cite{ma2024thinkongraph,sun2025searchongraph,liang2025fast}, iteratively expands a frontier of candidate triples, invokes an LLM as a relevance \emph{judge} to score each candidate, prunes to a fixed beam, and repeats for a predetermined number of hops. The dominant cost of this loop is the LLM judge: one call per hop, each consuming input tokens proportional to the candidate set.

Because questions vary in difficulty---a one-hop lookup versus a three-hop composition---a natural and widely pursued hypothesis is that a \emph{query-adaptive} controller can cut cost by spending expensive retrieval only where it helps. Recent work trains routers or reinforcement-learned policies for this purpose~\cite{dong2026graph,fan2026graphragrouter}, and an appealing alternative is to adapt \emph{without training}, using signals the pipeline already computes (entity-linking confidences, hop-level relevance-score distributions). Training-free adaptation would preserve GraphRAG's plug-and-play promise and transfer across KGs for free. This paper asks a simple question: \emph{does it actually work?}

\paragraph{This paper: a rigorous negative answer, and why.}
We began by building exactly such a training-free controller and testing it honestly against tuned baselines. It failed to enter the Pareto frontier. Rather than report a single failed method, we turned the investigation into a systematic study of \emph{whether training-free per-query adaptation can help GraphRAG at all}. We decompose adaptation into three orthogonal axes, each targeting a different lever of the retrieval loop:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item \textbf{Depth}: how many hops to run per query (adapt $K$).
  \item \textbf{Judge budget}: whether to invoke the expensive LLM judge at a hop, or fall back to a free embedding judge.
  \item \textbf{Judge type}: which relevance scorer---embedding similarity or LLM---to trust for a given query.
\end{itemize}
For each axis we construct the strongest training-free adaptive policies we can (signal-thresholded rules, patience/margin stopping, gap-based routing, and trained routers on question and pipeline features), and compare them against a \emph{tuned global} budget on the same query subset.

\paragraph{Finding 1: tuned global budgets dominate.}
On every axis, on two KGQA benchmarks (MetaQA~\cite{zhang2017variational}, ComplexWebQuestions~\cite{talmor2018knowledgebase}) and three judge scales (1.5B/7B/14B), a tuned global budget lies on or above the accuracy--cost Pareto frontier of every per-query adaptive policy we build---including an \emph{oracle-depth} baseline that cheats by using the true hop count. Adaptive depth loses to a tuned Fixed-$K$; adaptive judge budget loses to always-on or always-off; adaptive judge type loses to picking the better judge globally.

\paragraph{Finding 2: the upside exists but is unreachable---a predictability ceiling.}
To test whether this is a failure of \emph{our} policies or a fundamental limit, we run an oracle analysis. An omniscient per-query policy that always makes the optimal choice has a large upside (e.g., per-query judge selection raises F1 from $0.41$ to $0.47$ using only $27\%$ of LLM calls). Yet no realizable policy captures it: gap-based routing, threshold rules, and trained routers all fall \emph{below} the always-on baseline. We argue this is a \emph{predictability ceiling}: the quantity that determines the optimal per-query decision---whether an extra hop or a stronger judge would actually change the answer---is revealed only \emph{after} the expensive computation is run, so no inference-time signal computed \emph{before} it can anticipate the decision. Difficulty is observable post hoc, not predictable a priori.

\paragraph{Finding 3: the LLM judge is often unnecessary.}
A corollary with immediate practical value: a free embedding judge matches or exceeds the LLM judge on three of four depth regimes we test (it wins at 1-hop and on the denser CWQ graph, and only clearly helps at MetaQA 2-hop). This questions the default use of LLM relevance scoring in GraphRAG and yields a simple, strong, and cheap baseline.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
  \item We formalize training-free GraphRAG adaptation along three orthogonal axes (depth, judge budget, judge type) and evaluate the strongest training-free policy on each against tuned global budgets, on two benchmarks and three judge scales, with a shared retrieval engine so the policy is the only variable (\Cref{sec:method,sec:exp}).
  \item We establish a consistent negative result: tuned global budgets are Pareto-optimal or better on every axis, dominating even oracle-depth (\Cref{sec:exp-depth,sec:exp-judge}).
  \item We introduce an oracle analysis showing the adaptation upside is large but unrealizable, and articulate the \emph{predictability ceiling} that explains why (\Cref{sec:exp-oracle}).
  \item We show a free embedding judge is a strong baseline that questions the necessity of LLM relevance scoring, and give an actionable deployment recommendation (\Cref{sec:exp-judge,sec:discussion}).
\end{itemize}
