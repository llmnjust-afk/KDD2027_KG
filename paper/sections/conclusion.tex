\section{Discussion and Recommendations}
\label{sec:discussion}

Our results carry three practical implications for GraphRAG practitioners. First, \emph{tune one global budget rather than building a per-query adaptive controller}: across three adaptation axes, three benchmarks, and three judge scales, a small validation sweep over $(K,B,\text{judge})$ yields a policy that no training-free adaptive method we tested can dominate. Second, \emph{invest in the retrieval engine, not in per-query control}: our strong-engine ablation shows that a ToG-2.0-style embedding prefilter---which simply selects better candidates for the judge---improves accuracy far more reliably than any adaptation policy (e.g., the LLM judge's CWQ F1 rises from $0.024$ to $0.085$), and with fair candidate selection the LLM judge is worth its cost in all but the trivial 1-hop regime. Third, \emph{if adaptation is desired, it must use post-retrieval signals and likely learning}: the oracle upside is real, but capturing it requires information available only after running the expensive step, so a useful adaptive policy would need to speculatively execute and revise, or learn from post-hoc outcomes---not read pre-computed inference-time signals.

\paragraph{Scope and threats to validity.}
Our conclusions are established on two KGQA benchmarks with open-weight judges up to 14B; frontier judges or agentic multi-tool pipelines may shift the embedding-vs-LLM balance, though the predictability argument is orthogonal to judge strength. We study retrieval-budget adaptation, not answer-generation adaptation. The predictability ceiling is an empirical claim supported by the failure of a broad family of signals and trained routers; we do not prove impossibility, and a richer feature set could in principle do better---but the consistency across axes and datasets suggests the barrier is structural, not a lack of feature engineering.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked whether training-free, query-adaptive control can make GraphRAG cheaper without hurting accuracy, and answered it rigorously across three orthogonal axes. The answer is negative and consistent: tuned global budgets are Pareto-optimal or better everywhere, dominating even an oracle that knows the true hop count. An oracle analysis shows the per-query adaptation upside is large but unrealizable---no inference-time signal or trained router approaches it---because the information determining the optimal choice is revealed only after the expensive computation, a \emph{predictability ceiling}. A strong-engine ablation confirms the result is not an artifact of a weak retrieval engine: improving candidate selection helps far more than any adaptation policy, and does so uniformly rather than per query. We hope this study redirects effort from inference-time heuristics toward learned, post-retrieval adaptation and better retrieval engines, and provides a tuned-global-budget baseline and reproducible testbed for future work.
