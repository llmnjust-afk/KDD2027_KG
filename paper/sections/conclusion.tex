\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented \method{}, a training-free, query-adaptive retrieval controller for GraphRAG that decides per query whether to use the graph, how wide the beam should be, and when to stop expanding---using only distributional signals (link confidence, hop-score entropy, top-score trajectory) that the retrieval pipeline already computes and discards. Because \method{} and the fixed-budget baseline share one retrieval engine, our ablations isolate each controller component by construction.

The central empirical finding is that on the setting where GraphRAG is most valuable---multi-hop reasoning with a sufficiently strong judge---\method{} strictly Pareto-dominates the fixed ToG-style baseline: at 2-hop with a 7B generator it simultaneously improves F1 by $10.3\%$, Hit@1 by $7.0\%$, and reduces retrieval cost by $23.2\%$ ($p{=}0.049$). The advantage grows with reasoning difficulty (1.5B/2-hop: F1 $+23.5\%$, $p{=}0.004$) and the cost savings are unconditional, matching the theoretical cost bound. Ablations show early stopping is a free lunch at this operating point---it cuts cost \emph{and} raises accuracy by pruning noisy late hops---while adaptive beam contributes when hop scores are uncertain. A noise-corruption study confirms the use-graph decision fires precisely when entity linking is unreliable, saving up to $31\%$ cost.

We reported honest boundaries: on trivial single-hop queries the controller trades a small accuracy loss for cost savings, and on weak-judge high-depth settings graph retrieval itself becomes harmful. These limits are consistent with the theory---cost savings are guaranteed, accuracy gains depend on whether pruned hops carry signal or noise---and they make the positive results more credible rather than less. The practical implication is that \method{} is a zero-training, drop-in controller that any GraphRAG deployment can adopt, paying no accuracy cost where retrieval matters and saving substantial cost everywhere.

\paragraph{Future work.}
Three directions are promising. First, extending evaluation to larger KGs (Freebase, Wikidata) and frontier models would test whether the Pareto dominance scales beyond WikiMovies and 7B. Second, the controller's signals are derived from a single LLM serving as both judge and generator; decoupling a lightweight dedicated judge could sharpen the entropy signal and enable finer-grained budgeting. Third, the marginal-gain stopping rule assumes the top-score trajectory is a reliable proxy for evidence accumulation---exploring alternative stopping signals (e.g., answer-set convergence across hops, subgraph diversity) could improve robustness on KGs with denser, noisier neighborhoods. More broadly, the principle of training-free adaptation from pipeline-internal distributions may apply to other multi-step LLM pipelines beyond GraphRAG, such as agentic tool-use and multi-round reasoning.
