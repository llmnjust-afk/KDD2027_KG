\section{Discussion and Recommendations}
\label{sec:discussion}

Our results carry three practical implications for GraphRAG practitioners. First, \emph{tune one global budget rather than building a per-query adaptive controller}: across three adaptation axes, two benchmarks, and three judge scales, a small validation sweep over $(K,B,\text{judge})$ yields a policy that no training-free adaptive method we tested can dominate. Second, \emph{try the embedding judge first}: it is free and matches or beats the LLM judge in three of four depth regimes; the LLM judge earns its cost only when the task genuinely requires semantic disambiguation at an intermediate hop (MetaQA 2-hop in our study). Third, \emph{if adaptation is desired, it must use post-retrieval signals and likely learning}: the oracle upside is real, but capturing it requires information available only after running the expensive step, so a useful adaptive policy would need to speculatively execute and revise, or learn from post-hoc outcomes---not read pre-computed inference-time signals.

\paragraph{Scope and threats to validity.}
Our conclusions are established on two KGQA benchmarks with open-weight judges up to 14B; frontier judges or agentic multi-tool pipelines may shift the embedding-vs-LLM balance, though the predictability argument is orthogonal to judge strength. We study retrieval-budget adaptation, not answer-generation adaptation. The predictability ceiling is an empirical claim supported by the failure of a broad family of signals and trained routers; we do not prove impossibility, and a richer feature set could in principle do better---but the consistency across axes and datasets suggests the barrier is structural, not a lack of feature engineering.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked whether training-free, query-adaptive control can make GraphRAG cheaper without hurting accuracy, and answered it rigorously across three orthogonal axes. The answer is negative and consistent: tuned global budgets are Pareto-optimal or better everywhere, dominating even an oracle that knows the true hop count. An oracle analysis shows the per-query adaptation upside is large but unrealizable---no inference-time signal or trained router approaches it---because the information determining the optimal choice is revealed only after the expensive computation, a \emph{predictability ceiling}. Along the way we found that a free embedding judge matches or exceeds the LLM judge in most regimes, questioning a core cost of current GraphRAG systems. We hope this study redirects effort from inference-time heuristics toward learned, post-retrieval adaptation, and provides a strong, cheap, and honest baseline for future work.
