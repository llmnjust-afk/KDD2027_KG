\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Preliminaries: GraphRAG Retrieval}
\label{sec:prelim}

We formalize the GraphRAG retrieval loop that our controller augments. Given a knowledge graph $\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{R},\mathcal{T})$ with entities $\mathcal{E}$, relations $\mathcal{R}$, and triples $\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{E}\times\mathcal{R}\times\mathcal{E}$, and a natural-language question $q$, the goal is to retrieve a reasoning subgraph $\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{T}$ that, when provided as context to a generator LLM $G$, yields the correct answer.

The retrieval proceeds iteratively. Let $\mathcal{F}_k\subseteq\mathcal{E}$ denote the \emph{frontier}---entities to expand---at hop $k$. The pipeline consists of four steps per hop:

\textbf{(1) Entity linking} maps the question to seed entities $\mathcal{F}_0\subseteq\mathcal{E}$, either by exact match (when entity annotations are available) or by embedding nearest-neighbor search. Each linked entity carries a confidence score $\ell_i\in[0,1]$ (cosine similarity).

\textbf{(2) Frontier expansion} produces candidate triples by traversing one edge from each frontier entity: $\mathcal{C}_k = \{(e,r,e') \in \mathcal{T} : e \in \mathcal{F}_k,\; e' \notin \mathcal{V}\}$, where $\mathcal{V}$ is the set of already-visited entities. To bound prompt length, $|\mathcal{C}_k|$ is capped at $c_{\max}$.

\textbf{(3) Relevance judging} scores each candidate triple via an LLM call: $r_{k,j} = J(\text{triple}_j, q) \in [0,1]$. This is the \emph{dominant cost}: each call consumes input tokens proportional to $|\mathcal{C}_k|$, and there is one such call per hop. The judge prompt batches all candidates into a single call to amortize the fixed overhead of the instruction tokens.

\textbf{(4) Pruning and frontier update} retains the top-$b_k$ triples by score as $\mathcal{S}_k$, adds their entities to $\mathcal{V}$, and sets $\mathcal{F}_{k+1}$ to the newly discovered entities. After $K$ hops (or an early stop), the union $\bigcup_k \mathcal{S}_k$ is the reasoning subgraph fed to $G$.

A \emph{retrieval controller} $\pi$ governs three quantities per query: whether to use the graph at all ($u_0\in\{0,1\}$), the beam width ($b_k$), and whether to stop ($s_k\in\{0,1\}$). The fixed-budget baseline (ToG-style) sets $u_0{=}1$, $b_k{=}B$ constant, $s_k{=}0$ until $k{=}K$. \method{} replaces this with a training-free controller that reads distributional signals computed as by-products of steps (1)--(3). Because the retrieval engine (steps 1--4) is identical across controllers, comparing \method{} to the baseline isolates the controller as the sole variable.

\subsection{Distributional Signals}
\label{sec:signals}

\method{} exploits three signals that the pipeline already computes:

\textbf{(S1) Link-confidence vector.} Entity linking returns seed entities $e_1,\dots,e_m$ with cosine similarities $\bm{\ell}=(\ell_1,\dots,\ell_m)$ to the question embedding. The top value $\ell_{(1)}$ measures how reliably the seed anchor matches the question; a low $\ell_{(1)}$ signals that multi-hop expansion will amplify a wrong seed.

\textbf{(S2) Hop relevance distribution.} At hop $k$, the judge produces scores $\bm{r}_k=(r_{k,1},\dots,r_{k,|\mathcal{C}_k|})$ over candidates. We use its normalized entropy $H_k=-\sum_i \hat{r}_{k,i}\log\hat{r}_{k,i}/\log|\mathcal{C}_k|$ where $\hat{r}_{k,i}=r_{k,i}/\sum_j r_{k,j}$. Low $H_k$ means one candidate dominates (confident hop); high $H_k$ means scores are flat (uncertain hop, many plausible continuations).

\textbf{(S3) Per-hop top-score trajectory.} The sequence $t_k=\max_i r_{k,i}$ tracks whether expansion is uncovering more relevant evidence. A plateau or decline in $t_k$ indicates diminishing returns.

\subsection{The \method{} Controller}
\label{sec:controller}

\method{} makes three training-free decisions from (S1)--(S3).

\paragraph{(D1) Use-graph decision.}
If $\ell_{(1)}<\theta_{\text{low}}$, the seed anchor is too unreliable for multi-hop expansion to help; we route $q$ to a graph-free answer generated directly from the question. This saves all $K$ judge calls for that query. The threshold $\theta_{\text{low}}$ is the only hyperparameter governing (D1) and, as we show, is inert under perfect linking (it never fires) but decisive under noisy linking.

\paragraph{(D2) Adaptive beam allocation.}
We set the beam at hop $k$ to
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:beam}
b_k = \mathrm{clip}\!\Big(\big\lfloor B\cdot\big(\tfrac{1}{2}+H_k\big)\big\rfloor,\; b_{\min},\; b_{\max}\Big),
\end{equation}
where $B$ is a base beam. The factor $\tfrac{1}{2}+H_k$ maps entropy $H_k\in[0,1]$ to a multiplier in $[0.5,1.5]$: when scores are concentrated ($H_k\!\to\!0$) the beam shrinks to $B/2$ since one path suffices; when scores are flat ($H_k\!\to\!1$) the beam widens to $1.5B$ to avoid pruning the true continuation among many equally-scored candidates. This entropy-to-beam map is the simplest monotone allocation consistent with the intuition that uncertainty demands exploration.

\paragraph{(D3) Marginal-gain early stopping.}
We stop after hop $k\geq 1$ when the top-score gain fails to exceed a margin:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:stop}
s_k = 1 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \big(t_k - t_{k-1}\big) \le \delta \;\;\text{or}\;\; t_k < \tau \;\;\text{or}\;\; \mathcal{F}_{k+1}=\emptyset.
\end{equation}
The first condition halts when expansion stops improving the best available evidence (diminishing returns); the second is an absolute floor (nothing useful retrieved); the third is a structural frontier collapse. The default $\delta$ is chosen so that a hop whose top score does not beat the previous hop's top by a meaningful margin---indicating the new frontier is no more relevant than the old---is not worth its judge cost.

\paragraph{Assumptions on the signals, and their limits.}
Both (D2) and (D3) treat the raw LLM relevance scores as if they were meaningful across candidates and across hops, which requires care. The judge emits integers in $[0,100]$ that are \emph{not} calibrated probabilities, so the entropy $H_k$ in (D2) is a heuristic dispersion measure, not an information-theoretic quantity; we normalize per hop to reduce scale sensitivity, and when all scores are zero we fall back to the base beam $B$ (the normalization is undefined there). (D3) further assumes the per-hop top score $t_k$ is comparable across hops, which can fail: a necessary later-hop relation may have lower surface relevance than the first hop, so a score decrease does not strictly imply diminishing evidential value. We therefore do not claim these signals are optimal or calibrated; we claim only that they are \emph{informative enough} to drive useful budget decisions, and we validate this empirically---the sensitivity sweep (\Cref{fig:sensitivity}) shows the decisions are robust to the thresholds, and the 1.5B/3-hop boundary case (\Cref{sec:exp-boundary}) shows exactly where the assumption breaks: a weak judge produces an unreliable $t_k$ trajectory, so (D3) misfires. A calibrated judge or a learned score-to-decision map is a natural extension we leave to future work.

\begin{remark}[Call-count accounting]
\label{prop:cost}
Let $C_{\text{fix}}$ and $C_{\text{ada}}$ be the expected per-query judge-call count of the fixed and \method{} controllers under the same engine. If a fraction $\rho_u$ of queries satisfy (D1)'s skip condition and the mean stopping hop under (D3) is $\bar{k}_{\text{stop}}\le K$, then $C_{\text{ada}}=(1-\rho_u)\,\bar{k}_{\text{stop}}\le(1-\rho_u)\,K=(1-\rho_u)\,C_{\text{fix}}$.
\end{remark}
\noindent We state this as an accounting identity rather than a theorem: it follows directly from the definitions of (D1) and (D3) and merely quantifies the call-count reduction (skipping saves a factor $\rho_u$; early stopping saves $1-\bar{k}_{\text{stop}}/K$). It makes \emph{no} claim about accuracy, about the optimality of the stopping rule, or about token cost beyond call count (token cost tracks call count only up to per-hop candidate-count variation). Whether stopping preserves accuracy is an empirical question we answer in \Cref{sec:exp}: at 2-hop with a strong judge the skipped hops carry noise, so accuracy is preserved or improved, whereas at 1.5B/3-hop the stopping signal is unreliable and accuracy suffers (\Cref{sec:exp-boundary}). We deliberately avoid framing the stopping rule as theoretically justified; its justification is empirical.

\subsection{Why Training-Free Suffices}
\label{sec:why}

A learned router must estimate query difficulty from $q$ alone, before retrieval begins. \method{} instead observes difficulty \emph{during} retrieval through the score distributions the pipeline already produces. This has two consequences. First, the signal is causal rather than predictive: $H_k$ directly measures the judge's current uncertainty over actual candidates, not a proxy estimated from question features. A learned router that sees only $q$ cannot know whether hop $k$ will produce a confident or uncertain distribution---that depends on the KG's local neighborhood around the linked entities, which is only revealed during traversal. Second, the controller transfers for free across KGs and domains because it reads no domain-specific features---only normalized entropies and cosine similarities, which are defined wherever linking and judging are defined. A learned router trained on one KG's question-difficulty distribution may not transfer to a KG with different neighborhood density or relation semantics; \method{}'s signals are invariant to these properties because they are normalized.

\paragraph{Comparison to trained controllers.}
A trained controller like GraphRAG-Router~\cite{fan2026graphragrouter} optimizes a routing policy via RL to minimize a cost-accuracy objective. This can in principle learn richer patterns than our three hand-designed rules, at the cost of (i) a training stage requiring cost-accuracy labels or a simulator, (ii) a policy that may overfit to the training KG's difficulty distribution, and (iii) additional inference latency for the policy network. \method{} trades expressive power for zero training cost: it cannot learn patterns our three rules miss, but it works immediately on any KG without training data. Our experiments show this suffices to reach or exceed the cost--accuracy frontier of tuned fixed baselines at the operating points where GraphRAG matters most, suggesting that a substantial part of the achievable adaptation gain does not require learning---it requires reading signals that are already computed and discarded.

We verify robustness to these hyperparameters empirically by running the identical controller across both generator scales (1.5B and 7B) and all three reasoning depths without re-tuning: the same $\theta_{\text{low}}$, $\delta$, and $B$ values are used throughout. We emphasize that this establishes robustness across generators and depths \emph{on WikiMovies only}; transfer to structurally different KGs is not yet established and is left to future work (\Cref{sec:conclusion}).

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\method{} retrieval (training-free controller)}
\label{alg:aqurag}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require question $q$, KG $\mathcal{G}$, judge $J$, params $\theta_{\text{low}},B,\delta,\tau,K$
\State Link seeds: $\{(e_i,\ell_i)\}\leftarrow\textsc{Link}(q,\mathcal{G})$
\If{$\max_i \ell_i < \theta_{\text{low}}$} \Comment{D1: skip graph}
  \State \Return $\textsc{Generate}(q,\emptyset)$
\EndIf
\State $\mathcal{F}\leftarrow\{e_i\}$; $\mathcal{V}\leftarrow\emptyset$; $\mathcal{S}\leftarrow\emptyset$; $t_{-1}\leftarrow\bot$
\For{$k=0,\dots,K-1$}
  \State $\mathcal{C}\leftarrow\textsc{Expand}(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{G})\setminus\mathcal{V}$
  \State $\bm{r}\leftarrow J(\mathcal{C},q)$; \quad $H\leftarrow\textsc{NormEntropy}(\bm{r})$; \quad $t\leftarrow\max\bm{r}$
  \State $b\leftarrow\mathrm{clip}(\lfloor B(\tfrac12+H)\rfloor, b_{\min}, b_{\max})$ \Comment{D2}
  \State $\mathcal{S}_k\leftarrow\textsc{TopB}(\mathcal{C},\bm{r},b)$; \quad $\mathcal{S}\leftarrow\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{S}_k$
  \State $\mathcal{F}\leftarrow\textsc{Entities}(\mathcal{S}_k)\setminus\mathcal{V}$; \quad $\mathcal{V}\leftarrow\mathcal{V}\cup\mathcal{F}$
  \If{$k\geq 1\;\textbf{and}\;(t-t_{k-1}\le\delta\;\textbf{or}\;t<\tau\;\textbf{or}\;\mathcal{F}=\emptyset)$} \Comment{D3}
    \State \textbf{break}
  \EndIf
  \State $t_{k-1}\leftarrow t$
\EndFor
\State \Return $\textsc{Generate}(q,\mathcal{S})$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Complexity Analysis}
\label{sec:complexity}

GraphRAG cost is dominated by judge calls, each over up to $c_{\max}$ candidates. The fixed controller makes $K$ such calls plus one generation call. \method{} cuts this in three ways: (D1) eliminates all $K$ judge calls for the $\rho_u$ skipped queries; (D3) terminates at hop $k^*<K$, eliminating later calls; and (D2), while not changing the call count at a hop, shrinks the frontier and hence the candidate count at the next hop. Empirically (D3) is the primary driver (\Cref{tab:abl}). Critically, the controller adds \emph{zero} overhead: $H_k$, $t_k$, and $\ell_{(1)}$ are scalar reductions of arrays the pipeline already computed, negligible beside the LLM call that produced them---so \method{} is a pure decision policy with no learned parameters and no inference cost of its own.
